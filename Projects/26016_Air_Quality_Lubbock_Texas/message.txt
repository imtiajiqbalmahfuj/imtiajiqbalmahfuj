I. Research Framing: Methodology, Gap Analysis & Novelty

Background & Problem Statement

Semi-arid urban-agricultural interfaces, such as Lubbock, Texas, present highly complex atmospheric environments. Situated downwind of the Permian Basin (a major petroleum hub) and surrounded by intensive cotton agriculture and feedlots, the region is subjected to fugitive greenhouse gas emissions ($\text{CH}_4$), criteria combustion pollutants ($\text{NO}_2, \text{CO}$), and severe wind-driven dust resuspension (represented by Aerosol Optical Depth and $\text{UVAI}$). Traditional ground-based air quality monitoring relies on sparse point data, which fails to capture the spatial non-stationarity of these pollutants or their complex non-linear interactions with localized thermal drivers like the Surface Urban Heat Island (SUHI) effect.

Literature Review & Research Gap

Recent advancements in cloud-based Earth observation have significantly improved spatial pollution tracking. Sameh et al. (2026) utilized Sentinel-5P to map trace gases and evaluated their correlation with Land Surface Temperature (LST) via Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) in Egypt. Rahman et al. (2025) applied Mann-Kendall trend testing to monitor intra-annual pollution dynamics in Dhaka. Alvarez et al. (2025) introduced machine learning and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to predict urban air quality using satellite embeddings.

The Critical Gap: Previous studies evaluate pollutants in isolation or rely on global regression models that fail to explain why specific chemical and thermal interactions occur locally. Furthermore, black-box machine learning algorithms forecast pollution accurately but lack physical interpretability, leaving policymakers without actionable emission-source attribution.

Research Novelty & Objectives

This study bridges these gaps by synthesizing a multi-sensor satellite array (Sentinel-5P, MODIS, ERA5-Land) into a single analytical pipeline. The objectives are:

To generate continuous high-resolution spatiotemporal distributions (daily and monthly) for 12 critical atmospheric and thermal parameters.

To evaluate spatial non-stationarity using GWR, transitioning from traditional point-based metrics to continuous interpolated local $R^2$ zonal surfaces.

To deploy a Performance-Weighted Machine Learning Ensemble integrated with Explainable AI (SHAP) to predict $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ hotspots and attribute the exact physical drivers (thermal vs. synoptic) governing local air quality.

Clarification: ML Ensemble vs. SHAP (The "Why" and "What")

Machine Learning Ensemble (The "What"): Algorithms like Random Forest and Gradient Boosting are used to predict particulate matter concentrations with high accuracy because they capture complex, non-linear environmental interactions (e.g., how temperature, pressure, and $\text{NO}_2$ simultaneously trap dust). The ensemble fuses these models to maximize predictive accuracy and reduce error (RMSE).

SHAP (The "Why"): While the ML Ensemble predicts the pollution level, it operates as a "black box." SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) deconstructs this black box based on cooperative game theory. It calculates the exact mathematical contribution of each variable to a specific prediction (Alvarez et al., 2025). SHAP tells us why the model made its prediction—explicitly revealing whether a local pollution spike was driven by extreme heat ($\text{LST}$), stagnant weather (Surface Pressure), or combustion emissions ($\text{NO}_2$).

Together: The ML Ensemble defines the Hotspot Map, and SHAP provides the scientific justification for what causes the hotspot, allowing for targeted regulatory policy.

Methodological & SHAP Explanation (Based on Alvarez et al., 2025)

Why SHAP?

Machine learning models (such as Random Forest or Gradient Boosting) are highly effective at capturing non-linear relationships in environmental data, but they operate as "black boxes." You know what the model predicted, but not why it predicted it.

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), derived from cooperative game theory, resolves this by calculating the exact marginal contribution of each feature to a specific prediction (Alvarez et al., 2025). In this research, SHAP tells us precisely how much localized thermal anomalies (LST), synoptic weather (Pressure, Temperature), or trace gases ($\text{NO}_2, \text{CO}$) influence the accumulation of $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ at any given location. Rather than relying on simple correlations, SHAP isolates causality within complex, multi-variable environments, providing policy-makers with empirical evidence on which environmental drivers actually dictate localized pollution spikes.

Methodological Execution

Cloud-Native Data Fusion: We bypass traditional download limits by utilizing Google Earth Engine (GEE) to aggregate daily and monthly composites for 12 distinct atmospheric and thermal variables over Lubbock, Texas.

Temporal Trend Analytics: We generate both high-frequency daily trajectories and smoothed monthly trajectories, applying linear regressions to detect monotonic shifts across the calendar year (Rahman et al., 2025).

Spatial Non-Stationarity (GWR): Standard regressions assume relationships are uniform everywhere. We apply Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) and spatial interpolation to map how the statistical relationship between LST and pollutants physically changes across rural versus urban zones (Sameh et al., 2026).

Explainable GeoAI Modeling: We train multiple algorithms (Random Forest, SVR, Gradient Boosting) on the spatial pixels to predict localized pollution patterns. TreeSHAP is then applied to the champion model to deconstruct feature importance (Alvarez et al., 2025).

• Atmospheric constituents ($\text{CH}_4, \text{NO}_2, \text{SO}_2, \text{HCHO}, \text{O}_3, \text{UVAI}, \text{CO}$) are retrieved from Sentinel-5P L3 Offline products with quality assurance filtering ($\text{QA} > 0.75$) (Veefkind et al., 2012; Rahman et al., 2025).



• Land Surface Temperature ($\text{LST}$) is extracted from MODIS MOD11A1 (1 km resolution) and converted to Celsius. Surface Urban Heat Island (SUHI) intensity is calculated as pixel departures from the rural baseline (Wan et al., 2015; Sameh et al., 2026).

• Spatial Non-Stationarity: Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) with an adaptive Gaussian kernel estimates spatially varying parameter surfaces and local coefficient of determination ($R^2$) maps across Lubbock County (Fotheringham et al., 2002; Sameh et al., 2026).

The Research Framework: Spatiotemporal Dynamics and Explainable Predictive Modeling

Background and Context

Rapid urbanization, agricultural intensification, and fossil fuel extraction significantly alter land surface temperatures (LST) and degrade ambient air quality across semi-arid environments (Sameh et al., 2026; Rahman et al., 2025). The interactions between anthropogenic emissions and microclimatic parameters create severe localized pollution hotspots. In semi-arid regions, these atmospheric interactions are complicated by persistent dust resuspension, planetary boundary layer inversions, and high thermal forcing from impervious urban surfaces.

The Problem Statement

Air quality management in transitionary urban-agricultural regions suffers from severe observational deficits. Ground-based monitoring networks are extremely sparse and fail to capture the high-resolution spatial gradients of primary criteria pollutants and secondary trace gases. Furthermore, the mechanistic coupling between thermal anomalies (such as the Surface Urban Heat Island effect) and multi-pollutant accumulation remains poorly constrained across seasonal cycles, limiting the effectiveness of municipal mitigation policies (Alvarez et al., 2025; Sameh et al., 2026).

The Research Gap

Current environmental studies exhibit three critical vulnerabilities. First, research typically isolates a single pollutant dimension (e.g., exclusively particulate matter or greenhouse gases), ignoring the simultaneous chemical interplay among $\text{CH}_4, \text{NO}_2, \text{SO}_2, \text{O}_3$, and $\text{HCHO}$. Second, standard regression frameworks apply global assumptions to spatial data, disregarding the geographic non-stationarity of climatic relationships across diverse rural-urban land covers. Third, advanced predictive models often deploy machine learning as an opaque "black-box," successfully forecasting pollution levels but failing to explain the underlying physical drivers or meteorological thresholds responsible for those predictions.

The Novelty

This study resolves these limitations by engineering a comprehensive, cloud-native GeoAI pipeline. It fuses non-parametric temporal analysis (Mann-Kendall), local spatial statistics (Geographically Weighted Regression), and an explainable machine learning ensemble (SHAP) across a seven-pollutant spectrum. This approach translates massive multi-sensor satellite arrays into decipherable, policy-ready climatic intelligence without relying on manual ground measurements.

Objectives

Quantify the spatiotemporal distribution, dual-diurnal thermal regimes, and intra-annual monotonic trends of trace gases using Sentinel-5P and MODIS data.

Evaluate spatial non-stationarity between land surface temperatures and atmospheric pollutants using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR).

Develop and validate a five-algorithm machine learning framework (SVR, Ridge, RF, GB, KNN) coupled with Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to predict particulate matter and attribute exact physical feature importance.

Study Area Justification

Lubbock, Texas ($33.5779^\circ\text{ N}, 101.8552^\circ\text{ W}$) serves as an optimal natural laboratory. Positioned on the semi-arid Llano Estacado, it combines a dense urban core with extensive surrounding cotton agriculture and concentrated animal feeding operations. Crucially, it sits directly downwind of the Permian Basin, one of the world's highest-emitting petroleum extraction zones. This unique geographic placement creates a highly complex, multi-source emission profile governed by severe convective wind regimes and extreme thermal variances.

Methodology and Execution

The research executes entirely within a Google Earth Engine Python API framework:

Data Acquisition: Cloud-native retrieval of Sentinel-5P (Trace Gases), MODIS (LST and AOD-derived PM), and ECMWF ERA5-Land (Meteorology).

Temporal Trend Analysis: Utilizing the Mann-Kendall test and Sen's Slope to statistically verify monotonic pollution trajectories across the calendar year (Rahman et al., 2025).

Spatial Analysis: Generating continuous grids of local $R^2$ using an adaptive Gaussian GWR kernel to measure how the influence of LST on trace gases changes geometrically (Sameh et al., 2026).

Predictive Modeling: Extracting 1,500 spatial sample points to train Support Vector Regression, Ridge, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and KNN algorithms. The champion model undergoes TreeSHAP/KernelSHAP decomposition to isolate the marginal contribution of individual meteorological features (Alvarez et al., 2025).

Expected Outcomes

The research will yield a high-fidelity cartographic atlas containing over 20 publication-grade maps documenting monthly pollution evolution, SUHI intensity, and local regression surfaces. Additionally, the quantitative pipeline will produce structured statistical ledgers detailing PCA factor loadings, model validation metrics (RMSE, $R^2$), and SHAP attribution scores, providing local authorities with an empirical blueprint for targeted environmental interventions.
